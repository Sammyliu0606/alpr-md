It’s Saturday morning. You wake up in your apartment in Hanover, Maryland, and decide to catch an early movie at Arundel Mills mall. It’s just over a mile away, a seven-minute drive.

On the way to the theater, cameras photograph your license plate three times.

Two cameras catch you as you leave your neighborhood, and once as you enter the mall.

They capture you four more times on the way back.

Seven photos are stored in a police database. Together, they show when you left, where you went and when you returned.

This is just the handful of automated license plate cameras deployed across Maryland.

Maryland police license plate cameras logged 4.8 billion scans in 2025. Locals want to know where the data is going
By Sammy Liu and Jake Tiger

Thousands of automated license plate reading cameras installed on poles and patrol vehicles throughout Maryland are photographing every car that passes by. The photos are converted to data – plate number, date, time, location and vehicle’s model – and stored in rapidly growing databases, which totaled at least 4.8 billion readings across 26 Maryland law enforcement agencies in 2025, according to a Capital News Service analysis of state reports. As more agencies have deployed automated license plate readers, known as ALPRs, to help with daily tasks, the annual number of readings has more than tripled since 2022.

Databases are swelling as agencies add cameras to Maryland’s traffic hotspots. From 2022 to 2025, Montgomery County Police expanded its network from 30 cameras to 860, Harford County Sheriff’s Office from 18 to 274 and Anne Arundel County Police from 94 to 314.

In Maryland, how long the data is kept and what it takes for other agencies to obtain it depends largely on each agency's own policies, which vary widely according to reports from the Maryland Department of State Police.

The CNS analysis is based on annual License Plate Reader Program reports that every Maryland agency capturing and storing ALPR data must submit under the state’s Public Safety Article, Section 3-509.

Police officers across the state have been using databases compiled from these cameras’ photos to help with investigations.

Anne Arundel County Police Department says the data is a source of “valuable leads” that helps officers “quickly identify and locate” vehicles and people tied to crimes, according to the department’s website.

The Baltimore Police Department has an additional 220 ALPR cameras available to use after the city approved a $153 million bundled contract this year with Axon, the Arizona-based company best known for Tasers and police body cameras. Baltimore police told CNS through email that the technology helps achieve a nearly 70% recovery rate on stolen autos and gets investigators actionable information faster.

Salisbury Mayor Randy Taylor earlier this year called the cameras a “force multiplier” for investigations, as the city inked a $1.1 million, 40-camera contract with Atlanta-based Flock Safety this year, The Baltimore Sun reported.

But some residents feel like they’re being watched. Critics have said the cameras’ indiscriminate, nonstop data collection has crossed a line.

Nancy Meyer, who has lived in Hyattsville for 33 years, has watched cameras go up in her community. “Personally, I am not comfortable with cameras,” she said, adding that her neighbors and friends feel “apprehensive” about having their daily movements monitored.

“Folks don’t really know what the city or the vendors are doing with the information," Meyer said.

Meyer has become involved with a group of Hyattsville residents who formed DeFlock HVL, calling for the removal of all cameras in the city. Some residents have called the network “a clear violation of their Fourth Amendment rights,” said Alex Koch, the organizer of DeFlock HVL.

The exact locations of ALPR cameras deployed are not made publicly available by law enforcement agencies. People have instead created crowdsourced maps, like DeFlock Maps, where volunteers report the locations and vendors of cameras they spot, though some cameras may be misidentified or may have been removed since they were recorded.

Maryland strengthened its data privacy laws in July as federal agencies were using the data for immigration enforcement. The new protections prevent the selling of personal data, including ALPR scans, to groups like Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Previously, private data brokers and state and local agencies could sell data – including Motor Vehicle Administration records and school enrollment data – to ICE without a warrant, according to advocacy organization We Are CASA.

State lawmakers have discussed additional regulations specifically for ALPRs during next year’s legislative session, two Maryland delegates told CNS.

The troves of location data held by law enforcement have raised public concerns about surveillance. A growing number of reported misuse cases show how the data can be abused, from officers stalking ex-partners and tracking a woman who had an abortion to data shared with federal immigration authorities without the knowledge of those being tracked.

Salisbury cited misuse cases as a reason for launching its Flock transparency portal in August, which includes a running audit of the department’s searches in the last 30 days.

Salisbury Police Chief Dave Meienschein told WMDT last month that each search in the audits would include officer identification and a case number, but current Salisbury Flock reports do not include either, CNS found.

The Salisbury Police Department did not respond to a request for comment.

Maryland has no statewide limit on how long law enforcement agencies can keep ALPR data that isn’t connected to a crime. Depending on the agency, some delete it after 30 days; others keep it for 90 days to a year. And even agencies with short retention periods transfer partial or complete datasets to agencies that keep the data for up to a year, according to a CNS analysis of Maryland Department of State Police reports on ALPR usage and policies from individual police departments posted on their websites.

One such agency is the Maryland Coordination and Analysis Center, a state fusion center that pools data from municipal and county police to analyze crime and alert police when a plate on a watch list is spotted.

In August, Flock updated its data-retention recommendation from 30 days to seven days citing misuse by law enforcement, but the recommendation is just that. Local police departments would have to amend their policies to incorporate the new recommendation, according to Flock. Salisbury and Anne Arundel County, for example, still hold data for 30 days, according to their transparency portals.

Although state law requires a “legitimate law enforcement purpose” to share ALPR data with outside agencies, it sets no standard for what requesting agencies must provide to prove it.

Some agencies, such as Montgomery County and Capitol Heights police, require a case number for requests, according to the agencies’ policy documents. Prince George’s County’s policy specifies no such requirement, according to policy documents obtained by the nonprofit MuckRock, and the department has granted every request since at least 2023, according to state reports. In 2025, it approved all 11,104 requests, including 12 from out of state and 69 from federal agencies.

Prince George's Police Department did not respond to a request for comment.

Opponents of the technology argue that collecting data on all people — regardless of their relevance to an active investigation — is effectively a police search without a warrant, violating Fourth Amendment protections. That’s the position of the Electronic Privacy Information Center, a research foundation based in Washington that has wrestled with the cameras for years.

Maria Villegas Bravo, a lawyer for EPIC, told CNS, “Your location data is some of the most sensitive information about you. It can tell people whether you're going to church, whether you're going to a doctor's office.”

Questions about the technology’s place in law enforcement have even bubbled up within the industry. Charles Degliomini, an executive vice president at Columbia-based ALPR company Rekor Systems has called for regulation that limits who police can track.

“Every day you read about a police officer being arrested or fired or both for misusing the system,” he said in an interview with CNS. “That's what we think needs to change.”

As a Marylander himself, Degliomini has seen the new cameras spring up on his commute from Baltimore to Columbia. It’s something many others in the state have experienced over the last few years, as familiar highways and back roads have been dotted with solar-powered cameras.

Maryland Del. Sarah Wolek, D-Montgomery, who co-sponsored the state’s anti-ICE data privacy bill earlier this year, told CNS she will be bringing forward a bill in the upcoming legislative session that regulates ALPR technology. When asked if she would consider a seven-day legal limit on data retention that aligns with Flock's recommendation, she said she was “looking at everything.”

“Having advancements in technology doesn't mean that we are making progress as a society and that we need to be really thoughtful about how we're incorporating those advancements into governance,” Wolek said.

Another co-sponsor of state privacy protections, Del. Lorig Charkoudian, D-Montgomery, similarly said, “There's conversations that are happening” on ALPR regulation for Maryland

In speaking with her constituents, Wolek believes Marylanders are OK with cameras that have clearly defined purposes that make their communities safer, like speeding and red-light cameras. Marylanders have taken issue with ALPRs because they see them increasingly often — whether it be on their morning commute or a trip to the movies — but it’s unclear where the information is going and how it’s being used.
